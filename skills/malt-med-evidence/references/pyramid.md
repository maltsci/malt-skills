# Traditional Evidence Pyramid

Teaching hierarchy of study designs by typical potential to support causal inference. **Not** a final quality verdict for any individual paper.

## Tiers (top → bottom)

| Tier label (use in reports) | Typical designs | Best suited for | Main limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Apex | Systematic review / meta-analysis | Aggregating similar studies; overall effect estimate | Can still be unreliable if included studies are poor or question is wrong |
| High | Randomized controlled trial (RCT) | Therapy / prevention causal effects | Cost, generalizability, ethics constraints |
| Mid-high | Cohort study | Exposure → future outcomes; prognosis; some harms | Confounding, selection |
| Mid | Case-control | Rare outcomes; risk factors | Selection bias, recall bias |
| Descriptive | Cross-sectional | Prevalence / snapshot associations | Temporality unclear |
| Signal | Case series / case report | Rare events, signals, hypotheses | No adequate control; cannot prove efficacy |
| Preclinical | Animal / in vitro | Mechanism, early safety signals | Not direct human clinical benefit |
| Opinion | Expert opinion / narrative viewpoint | Experience when evidence is sparse | Subjective; conflicts of interest |

## How to assign a pyramid tier

1. Identify the **primary design** of the paper or evidence package (not the journal prestige).
2. Map to the nearest tier above.
3. If the paper is a systematic review of low-quality studies, still label **Apex** for design class, but state in rationale that apex design ≠ reliable conclusion.
4. Never treat pyramid height as interchangeable with Oxford Level, GRADE certainty, or OpenEvidence-style letters.

## Limitations (always keep visible)

- A well-conducted cohort can outrank a poor RCT for decision usefulness.
- Some questions (harm, prognosis, diagnosis) are poorly served by forcing “RCT at the top.”
- Pyramid is a **first screen**, not a guideline panel judgment.
